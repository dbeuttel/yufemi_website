What it is

It's not moss. It's a seaweed.

Sea moss is a red seaweed: a type of algae that grows in the ocean, usually attached to rocks or ropes in shallow, moving water. It got the name "moss" from its bushy, frilly look. It has no roots, leaves or flowers; it absorbs everything it needs directly from seawater and sunlight.

In stores you'll usually find it in three forms:

  • Dried: crunchy, tangled strands that look like dried noodles. You soak and blend them yourself.
  • Gel: dried sea moss that's been soaked, rinsed and blended with water into a smooth, jelly-like paste. This is the most popular form, and it's what Yufemi makes.
  • Capsules, gummies & powders: processed into supplement form.

Types

Two seaweeds share one name.

"Sea moss" is a market name, not a single plant. Two main groups are sold under it, and they're different species.

Irish moss

Chondrus crispus

  • Water: cold North Atlantic
  • Where: rocky coasts of Ireland, the UK, France, Iceland, and Canada down to New England
  • Grows on: rocks and boulders, from the low-tide line down to about 20 m
  • Looks like: short, flat, fan-shaped fronds; dark red to purple
  • Harvest: almost always wild, by hand rake or gathered after storms

Caribbean "gold" sea moss

usually Gracilaria species

  • Water: warm tropical seas
  • Where: St. Lucia, Jamaica, Grenada and other Caribbean and Latin American coasts
  • Grows on: rocks in the wild, or tied to ropes in the open ocean
  • Looks like: long, thin, stringy strands; golden to purple
  • Harvest: wild-picked, rope-farmed in the sea, or grown in pools or tanks

Good to know: both make a similar gel, and both are sold as "sea moss." If a brand says "Irish sea moss" but the strands are long, thin and golden, it's probably Gracilaria. A trustworthy seller will tell you the species and the country it comes from.

Where it grows & how it's harvested

From the ocean to your fridge.

  1. 🌊

    It grows

    Sea moss grows in shallow, clean, moving seawater with plenty of sunlight, clinging to rocks near the low-tide line. It absorbs minerals straight from the water, which is why where it grows affects what's in it.

  2. 🤲

    It's harvested

    Wild ("wildcrafted"): picked by hand or with rakes from natural beds, often at low tide; in Atlantic Canada it's even gathered after storms wash it ashore. Good harvesters cut it rather than rip it out so it regrows.
    Ocean-farmed: cuttings are tied to ropes or nets floating in the sea and harvested every few weeks.
    Pool-grown: grown in tanks or pools on land. Cheaper, but critics say it doesn't absorb the same range of ocean minerals.

  3. ☀️

    It's cleaned & dried

    The seaweed is rinsed of sand and shells and sun-dried until crunchy, so it can be stored and shipped without refrigeration.

  4. 🫙

    It becomes gel

    Makers soak the dried moss until it softens and swells, rinse it again, then blend it with fresh water (and, at Yufemi, real ingredients like elderberry and ginger) into a smooth gel. Gel is perishable, so it lives in the fridge.

What "wildcrafted" means: collected from where it grows naturally, not farmed. It says nothing about quality on its own, so look for the species, the origin, and ideally lab testing.

A little history

Centuries in the kitchen.

  1. 1800s

    Ireland

    Coastal communities cooked Irish moss into puddings and warm milk drinks, and used it as a folk remedy for coughs and colds. It was one of the foods people turned to during the Great Famine (1845–1852).

  2. 1800s →

    The Caribbean

    The tradition traveled to the Caribbean, where "Irish moss" became a beloved drink, especially in Jamaica: sea moss simmered with milk, vanilla, cinnamon and nutmeg, often with peanut or oats.

  3. 1900s

    The food industry

    Scientists extracted carrageenan, the natural thickener that makes sea moss gel, from Irish moss. It's now used in ice cream, plant milks, toothpaste and more. The word comes from carraigín, Irish for "little rock," a traditional name for the seaweed.

  4. Today

    Wellness trend

    Social media turned sea moss gel into a wellness staple, with plenty of real nutrition and plenty of hype. Which brings us to…

What's inside

Small amounts of a lot of good things.

Sea moss is mostly water and a type of soluble fiber (the carrageenan that makes it gel). Like most seaweeds, it also carries minerals it absorbs from seawater:

  • Iodine needed to make thyroid hormones. The headline nutrient, and the one to respect (see Safety).
  • Calcium & magnesium for bones, muscles and nerves.
  • Iron which helps your blood carry oxygen.
  • Potassium for fluid balance and heart rhythm.
  • Folate & small amounts of other vitamins
  • Soluble fiber that can feed healthy gut bacteria.

How much of each depends heavily on the species, where it grew and the batch, so treat any exact numbers you see online with caution.

Benefits & the science

Why people take it, and what we actually know.

Most people use sea moss as an easy, natural way to add minerals and fiber to their day. Here's an honest scorecard of the most popular reasons. Most research so far is in the lab or in animals, not large human studies.

Well established Some early evidence Mostly anecdotal

Source of iodine

Well established

Seaweed is one of nature's richest iodine sources, and your thyroid needs iodine to work. The catch: too much can also upset the thyroid, so a little goes a long way.

Adds minerals & fiber

Well established

It genuinely contains minerals and soluble fiber. In the small amounts people eat, think of it as a top-up to a balanced diet, not a replacement for one.

Gut health

Early evidence

Seaweed fibers can act as "prebiotics" that feed good gut bacteria in lab and animal studies. Promising, but human research is limited.

Immune support

Early evidence

Compounds in red seaweeds show antioxidant and immune activity in the lab and in animals. That's not the same as proof in people. (Our blend also includes elderberry and ginger, which people have long used for the same reason.)

Skin

Mostly anecdotal

Sea moss is popular in face masks because the gel is hydrating and soothing. There's little research on eating it for better skin.

Energy, mood & more

Mostly anecdotal

Many people say they feel better taking it, and some of that may come from building a healthy morning routine. There aren't good studies on energy or mood yet.

Myth check: "92 of the 102 minerals your body needs"

You'll see this claim everywhere. It isn't backed by scientific evidence. Sea moss does contain a broad range of minerals, but in amounts that vary a lot, and no single food covers everything you need.

Safety

Enjoy it, and respect it.

Sea moss is a food, and for most healthy adults a spoonful a day is a reasonable way to enjoy it. A few things are worth knowing:

  • Iodine adds up. Seaweeds vary enormously in iodine, and US health guidelines set an upper limit of 1,100 micrograms a day for adults. Stick to a small daily amount and don't stack it with kelp or iodine supplements.
  • Thyroid conditions. If you have hypo- or hyperthyroidism, or take thyroid medication, talk to your doctor first.
  • Pregnant or nursing? Check with your OB-GYN or provider before adding any new supplement.
  • Medications. If you take prescription medicine, especially for thyroid, blood thinning, or kidney or heart conditions, ask your doctor or pharmacist.
  • Where it comes from matters. Seaweed absorbs what's in its water, including heavy metals in polluted areas. Choose sellers who can tell you the origin and, ideally, share lab test results.
  • Start small. Some people notice digestive changes at first. Begin with a teaspoon or tablespoon and see how you feel.

From moss to gel

Why most people buy it ready-made.

Making it yourself

  1. Rinse dried sea moss several times to remove sand and salt.
  2. Soak in clean water for 12–24 hours until it swells and softens.
  3. Rinse again, then blend with fresh water until silky.
  4. Refrigerate in an airtight jar. It thickens as it chills.

Takes a day, a good blender and some trial and error.

Quick answers

Sea moss FAQ

Is sea moss actually a moss?

No. Sea moss is a red seaweed (a type of algae) that grows in the ocean. The name comes from its bushy, moss-like look.

What's the difference between Irish moss and sea moss?

Irish moss usually means Chondrus crispus, a cold-water seaweed from North Atlantic rocky shores. Much of the sea moss sold today, especially gold Caribbean sea moss, is a different red seaweed, usually from the Gracilaria family, grown in warm water. Both are used to make sea moss gel.

What does sea moss taste like?

Well-cleaned sea moss gel tastes very mild, slightly salty or of the ocean, and has almost no flavor once blended into a smoothie or drink. Flavored gels, like elderberry ginger, mostly taste of their added ingredients.

How much sea moss should I have?

There's no official recommended amount. Because sea moss contains iodine, more is not better. Most people start with about a tablespoon of gel a day. Check with a healthcare provider if you have a thyroid condition, are pregnant or nursing, or take prescription medication.
